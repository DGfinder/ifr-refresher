# Administrational

Your instrument rating, IPC, recent experience and aircraft equipment answer different questions. For a proposed flight, work through all of them: what you are authorised to do, whether the relevant check remains valid, whether your recent experience covers the operation, and whether the aircraft can support it.

## Part 61 Definitions

**Source: page 6.** The cheat sheet lists the aircraft categories as aeroplane, helicopter, powered-lift aircraft, gyroplane and airship. It separately lists the classes: single-engine aeroplane, multi-engine aeroplane, single-engine helicopter, powered-lift aircraft, single-engine gyroplane and airship.

It includes this qualification:

> For this Part, the single-engine aeroplane class includes:
>
> (a) multi-engine centre-line thrust aeroplanes; and
>
> (b) multi-engine aeroplanes that are prescribed by a legislative instrument under regulation 61.050 (Prescription of multi-engine aeroplanes included in single-engine class).

**What this means:** category and class are not interchangeable. Both a single-engine aeroplane and a conventional multi-engine aeroplane are in the aeroplane category. Do not replace “same category” in a recent-experience requirement with “same class”, or use engine count alone to decide the legal class of an unusual aircraft.

**Apply it:** You are reviewing a requirement that asks for an approach in an aircraft of the same category. Your record shows an approach in a single-engine aeroplane and the proposed flight is in a multi-engine aeroplane. Are those different categories?

No: both are aeroplanes. That answers the category question only. It does not, by itself, establish the class/type privileges, relevant check or other requirements for the proposed multi-engine flight.

**Explain it aloud:** What is the difference between aircraft category and aircraft class, and why must you preserve the exact word used in a requirement?

## Part 61 Manual of Standards

**Source: page 7, CASR 61.035.** The cheat sheet explains that CASA may issue a Manual of Standards for Part 61 covering matters relating to flight crew licences. Its list includes knowledge, training, flight tests, general operating competencies, flight reviews and instrument proficiency checks, as well as other specified checks and competencies.

**What this means:** a rule saying that you need a check and the standard against which the check is conducted serve different purposes. Preparing for an IPC involves being able to demonstrate the required skills and decisions, not merely identifying the date on which the previous check expires.

**Apply it:** You can calculate your IPC expiry correctly, but cannot explain how you would plan and fly an entry to a hold. Has the expiry calculation demonstrated the practical competency?

No. It answers an administrative question. The practical task still needs to be understood and demonstrated. Use the relevant standards to organise practice alongside the source topics in this workbook.

**Explain it aloud:** Where would you look for the standards relevant to an IPC, rather than relying solely on a summary of validity periods?

## Validity of an Instrument Proficiency Check

**Source: page 7, CASR 61.880 paragraph 3.** The cheat sheet gives the validity period as:

> From the period the holder passes the flight test to the end of the 12th month after the month in which the holder passes the flight test.

It also describes validity through successful participation in an operator's training and checking system for an IFR operation in the relevant aircraft. For early renewal, it states:

> If the holder successfully completes an instrument proficiency check for the relevant aircraft within 3 months before the validity of the existing check expires, then the instrument proficiency check is valid to the end of the 12th month after the validity of the existing check.

**What this means:** use the end-of-month wording, and determine whether the early-renewal provision applies. Do not automatically count 365 days from the check date. Keep any operator-system pathway tied to the relevant operation and aircraft; “I fly for an operator” is not the same fact as successfully participating in the applicable system.

### Apply it: ordinary validity and early renewal

**Situation A:** Assume the ordinary validity provision applies. You pass the relevant test on 12 April 2024. What end date follows from the source wording?

The twelfth month after April 2024 is April 2025, so the period ends on **30 April 2025**, not 12 April. This exercise addresses the date calculation, not whether all requirements for a particular flight are satisfied.

**Situation B:** An existing relevant check expires on 30 September 2025. You successfully complete the relevant IPC on 15 August 2025. Assume the source's early-renewal provision applies. What happens to the expiry?

The check is completed within the three months before the existing expiry. The source preserves the existing expiry basis: the new period ends **30 September 2026**. It does not simply restart from August and end in August 2026.

**Cockpit relevance:** identify expiry early enough to organise a check without disrupting planned flying. Then check recent experience separately; a valid IPC is not a complete answer to “can I conduct this flight?”

**Explain it aloud:** What are you counting from when the early-renewal provision applies?

## Privileges and Limitations of an Instrument Rating

**Source: page 8, CASR 61.855 and 61.860.** The cheat sheet describes the instrument rating as authorising IFR flight or VFR flight at night, subject to the stated limitations. It lists these conditions:

- The aircraft must be equipped for the kind of instrument approach conducted.
- For single-pilot IFR, the holder must at some time have passed the instrument-rating flight test in a single-pilot aircraft or completed an IPC in a single-pilot aircraft.
- For an instrument approach, the holder must have completed training in that approach and demonstrated competence to CASA or an examiner.
- Circling has its own qualifying pathways, set out below.

For circling, the source gives three alternatives:

- The instrument-rating flight test was passed within the previous 12 months and included a circling approach; **or**
- The most recent IPC included a circling approach. The source also includes an operator proficiency check that covers IFR operations and is conducted by a flight examiner holding an instrument-rating flight-test endorsement; that check must include circling; **or**
- The holder is successfully participating in an operator's training and checking system for an operation that includes circling approaches.

Keep the qualifying event, its conditions and the relevant time wording together.

**What this means:** having the rating does not make every approach in every equipped aircraft available to you. Match the proposed operation to both the pilot's qualifying experience and the aircraft's capability. Circling is not automatically included just because the approach has circling minima printed on it.

### Apply it: the approach changes

**Situation:** You planned a straight-in approach. Conditions lead you to consider circling instead. Your most recent IPC did not include circling; you have not passed a qualifying rating flight test within the previous 12 months, and no applicable operator-system pathway covers circling.

**Decision:** Does possessing a valid IPC establish the circling qualification described on this page?

No. None of the source's circling pathways in this scenario has been established. Reassess the available plan rather than treating the general IPC validity date as authorisation for every kind of approach. The operational lesson is to consider possible approach changes during preparation, not discover the qualification gap at the decision point.

**Explain it aloud:** What pilot and aircraft conditions would you check before changing the kind of approach you intend to conduct?

## Recent Experience Requirements

**Source: pages 9–10, CASR 61.870 and 61.875.** The source's general table specifies the following experience in an aircraft or approved flight simulation training device:

| Intended operation | Experience in the previous 90 days |
|---|---|
| Flight under the IFR | At least three instrument approach operations |
| Aircraft of a particular category | At least one instrument approach operation in the same category |
| A 2D approach | At least one 2D instrument approach |
| A 3D approach | At least one 3D instrument approach |
| Azimuth guidance | At least one 2D instrument approach using azimuth guidance |
| Course deviation indicator | At least one instrument approach using a CDI |

The cheat sheet says these general recent-experience requirements are considered met following a successful operator proficiency check covering IFR within the previous **three months**, or through successful participation in an operator's training and checking system for an IFR operation. Preserve the source's “90 days” and “three months” wording rather than treating them as interchangeable units.

The single-pilot requirement appears separately:

> (1) The holder of an instrument rating is authorised to pilot an aircraft under the IFR in a single-pilot operation only if the holder has conducted a flight or simulated flight under the IFR in a single-pilot operation within the previous 6 months.
>
> (2) For subregulation (1), the flight or simulated flight must:
>
> (a) have a duration of at least one hour; and
>
> (b) include at least one instrument approach or simulated instrument approach.

**What this means:** the total number of approaches is only one question. Match the record to the proposed category, approach/guidance requirements and single-pilot operation. Do not carry the general table's operator alternatives across to a separate requirement without establishing that they apply.

### Apply it: a valid IPC, but a different recent flying pattern

**Situation:** Assume your relevant IPC is valid and your general recent-experience requirements are met. All your flying in the previous seven months has been multi-crew. You have not completed a qualifying single-pilot IFR flight or simulated flight during that period. You now plan a single-pilot IFR flight.

**Decision:** Do the facts establish the separate six-month single-pilot requirement?

No. The general recent-experience table and IPC date do not supply the missing single-pilot experience. Identify this before committing to the flight and arrange the appropriate means of meeting the requirement. This scenario does not attempt to prescribe the legal form of a recency-restoration flight.

### Apply it: three approaches is not the whole answer

**Situation:** Your record contains three instrument approach operations within 90 days, all 3D. There is no applicable operator alternative in this exercise. You are considering a 2D operation and have no 2D approach in the previous 90 days.

The total satisfies the source's “three approaches” row, but the supplied facts do not satisfy its separate 2D row. Work through the applicable rows instead of stopping at the total.

**Explain it aloud:** Why can an IPC validity date and a total approach count both be satisfactory while the proposed flight still has a recent-experience gap?

## Aircraft Equipment

**Source: pages 11–13, Part 91 MOS references shown below.** Equipment suitability includes what is fitted, the conditions attached to it and whether it is operative for the intended flight.

### IFR instruments and supporting systems

Page 11 lists approved GNSS, attitude indicator (AI), airspeed indicator (ASI), altimeter (ALT), vertical speed indicator (VSI), turn-and-slip indication (T+S), directional gyro (DG), compass, outside air temperature (OAT) and clock. Its numbered qualifications are part of the list:

| Source item | Footnote numbers | Source footnote text |
|---|---|---|
| AI | 1, 7 | 1: Primary and alternate power supply unless independent of T+S or a second AI required. |
| ASI | 2, 3 | 2: Pitot heat required on at least one ASI. |
| ASI, ALT, VSI | 3 | 3: With alternate static source or a balanced pair of flush static ports. |
| T+S | 4, 5, 7 | 4: Slip only if a second AI is available. 5: Primary and alternate power supply unless independent of other gyroscopic instruments or a second AI required. |
| DG | 6, 7 | 6: Primary and alternate power supply unless independent of T+S or a second AI required. |
| AI, T+S, DG | 7 | 7: Must include equipment to show adequate power supply to gyroscopic instruments (if any). |
| Clock | 8 | 8: Can be worn. |

These are the cheat sheet's compressed footnotes, retained together with their item references. They are not an aircraft-specific installation assessment. The original list cites Part 91 MOS 26.08.

**Cockpit relevance:** being able to name the instruments is the beginning. Know where the required information comes from in the aircraft you are flying, how it is powered, how failure is indicated, and what capability remains after a failure. Two displayed indications should not be assumed to have independent sources merely because they appear in different places.

### Altitude alerting and assigned altitude

Page 12, citing Part 91 MOS 26.25, distinguishes two cases:

- Piston aircraft and unpressurised turbine aircraft above FL150 in controlled airspace, and pressurised turbine aircraft in any controlled airspace: the source requires the specified altitude-alerting equipment. It includes an assigned-altitude indicator, an approaching-selected-altitude alert, and a deviation alert that includes an aural warning.
- Other aircraft operating IFR in controlled airspace: the source specifies equipment that at least includes an assigned-altitude indicator.

**Apply it:** You are comparing two IFR flights in controlled airspace below FL150: one in a pressurised turbine aircraft and one in an unpressurised turbine aircraft. Does “both are below FL150” settle their equipment requirements?

No. The pressurised-turbine condition applies in any controlled airspace in the supplied text. The unpressurised example falls into the other-aircraft case at this level. Aircraft characteristics and operating environment both matter; altitude alone does not answer the question.

### Inoperative equipment

Page 12, citing Part 91 MOS 26.04, says equipment required for the flight must be operative unless another section provides otherwise, or the defect is approved as a permissible unserviceability for the aircraft and flight and the equipment is fitted or carried in accordance with that permissible unserviceability.

**Apply it:** An item of required equipment is inoperative. Someone says, “We have managed without it before.” Is that the exception described by the source?

No. Past experience of coping with a failure does not establish the applicable permission for dispatch. Identify the defect and the documented basis on which the aircraft may conduct this flight. Do not substitute confidence in handling an airborne failure for resolving a known preflight defect.

### Lighting

Page 13 covers these separate equipment topics:

| Source reference | Cheat-sheet coverage |
|---|---|
| 26.21 — cockpit and cabin lighting | Equipment, checklists and documents must be readable; lighting must be compatible with the equipment, allow markings to be read from a normal position with eyes shielded from direct/reflected light, and have variable intensity. Cabin lighting covers seatbelts, oxygen and normal/emergency exits. An independent portable light is listed for each flight crew member. |
| 26.22 — anti-collision lights | One red beacon, two white strobes, or a combination. |
| 26.23 — landing lights | When operating by night, at least one landing light. |
| 26.24 — navigation lights | When operating by night, must be fitted. |

**Cockpit relevance:** assess usability as well as whether a lamp illuminates. During night preparation, can you read the checklist and relevant markings from the operating position, adjust brightness and use the independent light? A brightly lit display does not necessarily make the rest of the cockpit readable.

**Explain it aloud:** For a known equipment defect, how would you distinguish a missing required capability from a defect with an applicable permitted means of operation?

## Bring the checks together

For a proposed flight, explain the evidence for each part separately: relevant privileges and qualifications; check validity; applicable recent experience; aircraft capability and serviceability. Then consider the actual approaches, airspace and operating conditions you may encounter.

The point is to make the checks specific to the flight. “My instrument rating is current” compresses several different questions into a phrase and can conceal a gap.
